The Rothschild family claims that sending gold by coach between countries risks loss: in wartime to enemy seizure, in peacetime to thieves, leading to the proposal of an alternative international banking system to avoid these dangers.

Mayer Amschel Rothschild instructs his five sons to establish banking houses in different key European centers— one in Paris, one in Vienna, one in London, and implying others including Frankfurt— to create a networked family business for secure money transfers.

Year 1810: Mayer Amschel created a formal partnership agreement with his sons, establishing the firm Mayer Amschel Rothschild & Söhne. This is the legal moment the "network" was codified.

1812: Mayer Amschel died. His will strictly laid out the rules for the family business: the brothers must work together, share profits, and maintain secrecy.

The Dispersion of the Sons (1798–1820s):

    1798: Nathan Mayer (the most famous son) had already moved to England (first Manchester, then London).

    1811: James was sent to Paris.

    1816–1820s: Salomon went to Vienna, and Carl went to Naples (implied by "others"), while Amschel stayed in Frankfurt.

The mechanism for money transfer in the Rothschild system involves sending a letter instead of physical gold; for example, from Frankfurt to London, instructing the London branch to pay the recipient, with the transaction offset by reciprocal loans from London back to Frankfurt, eliminating risks to life and assets.

Nations requiring international money transport will preferentially use the Rothschild banks because the letter-based system ensures safety compared to physical shipments.

The House of Warburg employs the same international banking tactics as the Rothschilds, as do other Jewish banking families, establishing networked operations across countries to facilitate influence.
