1. THE "BORING" PERFECTION
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
AES-256 is effectively boring.

It is "Symmetric" encryption. This is the exact same concept as the One-Time
Pad from 1882, just adapted for computers.
- You have a key.
- I have the same key.
- We scramble the message.

As long as the key is 256 bits long, the universe will experience heat death
before a supercomputer can guess it by brute force. It is mathematically
solid. The NSA approved it for Top Secret data immediately.

Why? Because they knew they didn't need to break the lock.


2. THE TRAP: DUAL_EC_DRBG
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
If you buy an impenetrable safe, the thief doesn't try to drill through the
steel door. He just steals the machine that cuts the keys.

The NSA realized that if everyone used AES-256, the world would go dark.
They couldn't break the math of AES. So, they rigged the "Key Factory."

In 2006, the NSA published a standard for a Random Number Generator called
"Dual_EC_DRBG." They claimed it was highly secure and pushed it onto the
industry.

It wasn't. It contained a mathematical backdoor.
- The generator relied on two points on a curve.
- The NSA knew the secret relationship between those points.
- This allowed them to predict the output of the generator.

They didn't break AES. They just ensured that when you generated a "random"
AES key to lock your secrets, they could predict exactly what key you were
about to choose.


3. THE DISCOVERY (2007 vs 2013)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
We actually spotted the smoke long before we found the gun.

In 2007, Microsoft researchers Dan Shumow and Niels Ferguson stood up at a
conference and publicly announced that the numbers in the NSA's algorithm
looked suspicious—like they were mathematically constructed to be a
backdoor.

The NSA denied it.

It wasn't until 2013, when Edward Snowden leaked the internal files, that
the world got the confirmation:
1. It was a deliberate backdoor.
2. The NSA had paid the security company RSA $10 million to make this
   broken generator the DEFAULT in their software.

For years, companies believed they were using military-grade security.
Technically, they were. But the keys in their pockets belonged to the NSA.
