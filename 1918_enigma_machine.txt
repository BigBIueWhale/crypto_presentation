=============================================================================
HISTORY: THE ONE-TIME PAD
FOCUS:     Origins & The Invention of XOR
=============================================================================

1. THE LOST INVENTOR (1882)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
The concept was first invented by a Sacramento banker named Frank Miller in
1882. He published a book titled "Telegraphic Code to Insure Privacy and
Secrecy in the Transmission of Telegrams."

Miller's version did not use XOR. It used modular addition with numbers.
He proposed a system where a list of random numbers (the key) was added to
the code numbers of a message. However, Miller was a banker, not a
mathematician, and his invention went largely unnoticed and was forgotten
for decades.


2. THE ENGINEER & THE XOR MOMENT (1917)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
The One-Time Pad was reinvented in 1917 by Gilbert Vernam, an engineer at
AT&T Bell Labs. This is the precise moment the algorithm became based on XOR.

Vernam was trying to encrypt teleprinter traffic (telegraphs that used paper
tapes with holes). Teleprinters used a 5-bit binary code (Baudot code).

Vernam realized that if he took a loop of paper tape containing random holes
(the key) and combined it with the message tape using electrical relays, he
could scramble the signal.
- The electrical logic he built was "Modulo 2 Addition."
- In modern computer science, we call this the XOR operation.

This was the first time binary logic was used to mix a random key with a
message.


3. THE MATHEMATICIAN & THE PROOF (1940s)
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
While Vernam built the machine, he didn't prove *why* it was unbreakable.
That happened during World War II.

Claude Shannon, the father of Information Theory, was working on encryption
for the Pentagon. In a classified report (declassified in 1949), he proved
mathematically that Vernam's system provided "Perfect Secrecy."

Shannon proved that as long as the key was random, as long as the message,
and never reused, the XOR operation destroyed 100% of the information,
making it theoretically impossible to crack, regardless of computing power.
